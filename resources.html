Notes

Practical ideas about customers, business systems, software and the decisions that connect them.

Customer Experience

Your business may be losing customers before they ever contact you.

Getting people to discover your business is only half the job.

Before I tell you that you need more marketing, I want to understand what happens after someone discovers your business.

Someone sees your post. They click your profile. They visit your website. They find your page.

Then what?

Can they understand what you offer? Can they tell whether it is for them? Can they see what to do next? Can they ask a question without having to move between three different platforms?

Every unnecessary step creates friction.

And friction is expensive.

A business can spend money getting attention while quietly losing people because the experience after that attention is poor.

This is the kind of friction I look for before I build anything.

Problems like this are often difficult to see from inside the business, because everyone working there already knows how it all fits together. A stranger does not.

Sometimes the problem is not the marketing.

It is the system behind the customer journey.

Enquiries may be coming through WhatsApp, Instagram and a website with no central place to manage them. Customers may have to repeat the same information. Booking may take several messages when it should take one. There may be steps between interest and payment that do not need to be there.

What it can cost Let me put a number on what this could be costing your business.
50,000 qualified prospects reach you over a quarter. At a 2% conversion rate that is 1,000 customers. At an average customer value of $100, that is $100,000 in revenue.
Now look at the same 50,000 prospects at 4%. That is 2,000 customers, which is 1,000 more customers from an audience you are already paying to reach, and another $100,000 in revenue opportunity.
Your website may be confusing. Your response time may be slow. Your follow-up may be inconsistent. There may be too many steps between a customer's interest and their payment.
Every one of those is a customer you are losing, and money you are leaving behind.
That is what not fixing it is costing you.
Now the cost of fixing it. If building the right system cost $5,000, and the problem was costing $100,000 in revenue opportunity, the important question is not simply "How much does the solution cost?"
The important question is: "What is it costing me to leave this problem unresolved?"
The $5,000 is an illustrative example of what a solution might cost. It is not a quote, and it is not a promise of what your business would recover.
Illustrative example, not a client result.

Those are problems software can solve.

The important part is identifying where the friction actually happens before deciding what to build. Once the problem is clear, I can work out whether it needs software at all, and what kind.

The question is not only:

"How do I get more people?"

The question I want answered is also:

"Once they arrive, how much work is the business making them do?"

Takeaway: More demand is not always the answer. Often the problem is the friction between attention and action, and that is the part of the system I look for first.

Business Systems

If the same task keeps happening, it has stopped being a one-off task.

A business starts with people doing things manually.

Someone answers the enquiry. Someone sends the quotation. Someone updates the spreadsheet. Someone reminds the customer. Someone checks the payment. Someone sends the follow-up.

At first, that is normal.

The problem starts when the business grows but the process stays exactly the same.

The same task happens every day. The same information gets copied between systems. The same message gets sent again. The same reminder gets forgotten. The same person becomes the bottleneck.

That is no longer just a workload problem.

It is a systems problem.

What it can cost Look at the repetitive work your team handles every year.
If your team processes 75,000 enquiries, orders or transactions and spends just 5 minutes manually handling each one, that's 6,250 hours of repetitive work.
At $15 per hour, that's $93,750 of working time going into a process that could potentially be removed, simplified or automated.
And that's before counting mistakes, delays, missed follow-ups or the work your team could have been doing instead.
That's what the process is costing you.
Illustrative calculation, not a client result.

When I look at a business process, I don't start by asking what app I can build.

I want to understand where your team is repeatedly spending time, where information is being copied from one place to another, where mistakes happen and where one person has become the bottleneck.

That means finding out what starts the process, what information comes in, what decisions get made, what happens over and over, and what genuinely needs a human.

Once I understand that, I can determine what the process actually needs. There are four possibilities:

Remove it.

Some tasks should not exist in the first place.

Simplify it.

Some processes are complicated because they were never designed properly.

Automate it.

Some repetitive work can happen without someone doing it manually every time.

Turn it into software.

Some processes are important enough, frequent enough or complex enough to justify a proper system.

This is where software becomes useful, and where I decide what is actually justified.

I am not building an app because an app sounds impressive.

I am building around a business process that is costing you time, creating errors or making it harder to serve customers.

That is a much better starting point than:

"We need an app."

Takeaway: I diagnose the process first. Then I build software around the parts that genuinely need it.

Digital Presence

Your website is part of your sales process.

A lot of businesses build websites because they believe they need a website.

Then the website becomes:

About us.

Our services.

Our history.

Our contact details.

When I build a website, I don't treat it as an online brochure.

I look at what the person arriving on the site needs to understand, what uncertainty they have and what they need to do next.

A potential customer is usually asking different questions.

What exactly do you do?

Is this for someone like me?

Can you solve my problem?

Why should I trust you?

How does it work?

What should I do next?

A useful website reduces the uncertainty between discovery and action.

That means the job is not simply to make the business look professional.

The job is to make the next decision easier.

If someone has to send a WhatsApp message just to understand what the business sells, the website is not doing enough.

If someone has to search through several pages to find the right service, there is friction.

If the next action is unclear, attention can disappear.

The math Look at the traffic already reaching your website.
Say 80,000 relevant visitors reach your site and 2% take the action you want. That's 1,600 enquiries or customers.
If the experience is strong enough to reach 5%, the same traffic produces 4,000.
That's 2,400 additional conversions from the traffic you already have.
At an average value of $50 per conversion, that's $120,000 in additional revenue opportunity from the same traffic.
You didn't need another 80,000 visitors to create that opportunity. You needed to stop losing as much of the attention you were already paying to acquire.
Illustrative figures to show the potential cost of friction, not a benchmark claim.

And sometimes the answer isn't another page.

It might be a better enquiry flow.

A booking system.

A customer portal.

An automated follow-up.

An integrated payment process.

Or another digital workflow entirely.

The website is only one part of a larger customer system.

The real question is:

"What does the customer need to do, and what is making that unnecessarily difficult?"

Once that is clear, I can build the right software around the journey.

A website does not need dozens of features to be useful.

It needs to answer the right questions and make the next action obvious.

Takeaway: A website is one part of the customer system. I start with what the customer needs to accomplish, then build the software that removes whatever gets in the way.
